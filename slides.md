<div class="chapter">
  <div class="rule"></div>
  <h1>The presentation is not a product tour. It is a sequence of belief changes.</h1>
  <p class="text-2xl">Jobs moves the audience from category familiarity, to dissatisfaction, to a new interface logic, to proof through live use.</p>
</div>

---

<div class="grid-2">
  <div class="frame shot-wide">
    <img src="./assets/source-slides/slide-02.png" alt="Keynote outline" />
  </div>
  <div>
    <div class="eyebrow">The spine</div>
    <h2>He controls attention by changing the question every few minutes</h2>
    <ul class="compact">
      <li><strong>Why now?</strong> Apple has changed industries before.</li>
      <li><strong>Why this?</strong> Existing smartphones are compromised.</li>
      <li><strong>Why believe?</strong> The interface removes the compromise.</li>
      <li><strong>Why buy?</strong> Every demo lands on a familiar daily job.</li>
    </ul>
    <div class="analysis">
      <h3>What he is doing</h3>
      <p>The outline is a persuasion ladder: establish authority, create tension, reveal a mechanism, then let the demo cash the claim.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

---

<div class="grid-2">
  <div>
    <div class="eyebrow">Move 1: historical permission</div>
    <h2>Before the phone appears, he earns the right to announce a category shift</h2>
    <div class="quote">“A revolutionary product comes along that changes everything.”</div>
    <p>Macintosh and iPod are not nostalgia. They are precedent: Apple has done this kind of discontinuity before.</p>
    <div class="analysis">
      <h3>Why it works</h3>
      <p>The audience is asked to judge the iPhone inside a lineage of successful breaks, not inside the current phone aisle.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="frame shot">
    <img src="./assets/source-slides/slide-57.png" alt="Macintosh iPod iPhone historical sequence" />
  </div>
</div>

---

<div class="grid-2">
  <div class="frame shot">
    <img src="./assets/source-slides/slide-58.png" alt="iPod phone internet communicator icons" />
  </div>
  <div>
    <div class="eyebrow">Move 2: the three-in-one reveal</div>
    <h2>The first big surprise is linguistic before it is visual</h2>
    <div class="quote">“An iPod, a phone... are you getting it?”</div>
    <p>He names three categories, repeats them, then collapses them. The audience participates in the reveal before seeing the device.</p>
    <div class="analysis">
      <h3>Mechanics</h3>
      <p>Power of three plus repetition creates a pattern, then the punchline breaks the pattern. The laugh is also comprehension.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

---

<div class="grid-2">
  <div>
    <div class="eyebrow">Move 3: frame the enemy</div>
    <h2>He makes the status quo look trapped by its own hardware</h2>
    <div class="quote">“They all have these keyboards...”</div>
    <p>The critique is not “competitors are ugly.” It is more structural: fixed buttons cannot adapt to software.</p>
    <div class="analysis">
      <h3>Why it works</h3>
      <p>Once the problem is framed as adaptability, every keyboard phone looks obsolete even before iPhone features are listed.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="frame shot">
    <img src="./assets/source-slides/slide-12.png" alt="Four smartphones and keyboard critique" />
  </div>
</div>

---

<div class="grid-2">
  <div class="frame shot-wide">
    <img src="./assets/source-slides/slide-39.png" alt="Smartphone category critique" />
  </div>
  <div>
    <div class="eyebrow">Positioning</div>
    <h2>The product occupies a white space he has just drawn</h2>
    <p>Regular phones are not smart enough. Smartphones are not easy enough. iPhone is presented as a leap across both axes.</p>
    <div class="analysis">
      <h3>What to copy</h3>
      <p>Do not start with features. Draw the market map so the desired product position becomes the obvious missing quadrant.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

---

<div class="grid-2">
  <div>
    <div class="eyebrow">Move 4: solve from first principles</div>
    <h2>The “giant screen” is introduced as a logical conclusion</h2>
    <div class="quote">“Get rid of all these buttons.”</div>
    <p>He walks the room from computers to phones: bitmap screen, pointing device, adaptable interface.</p>
    <div class="analysis">
      <h3>Why it works</h3>
      <p>The audience is not asked to accept a black-box invention. They are led through a familiar analogy until the new device feels derived.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="frame shot">
    <img src="./assets/source-slides/slide-15.png" alt="Giant screen solution" />

<div class="grid-2">

  <div>
    <div class="eyebrow">Contrast</div>
    <h2>The stylus joke is a strategic rejection, not just a laugh line</h2>
    <div class="quote">“Who wants a stylus?”</div>
    <p>The physical object becomes a symbol of old interaction debt: lose it, store it, manage it.</p>
    <div class="analysis">
      <h3>Why it works</h3>
      <p>He removes an expected answer before revealing the better answer. That makes multi-touch feel cleaner by comparison.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

---

<div class="grid-2">
  <div>
    <div class="eyebrow">Move 5: make the mechanism memorable</div>
    <h2>“Born with ten of them” turns technical input into common sense</h2>
    <p>Multi-touch could have been described as sensor density and gesture recognition. Jobs makes it human: fingers first, technology second.</p>
    <div class="analysis">
      <h3>What he is doing</h3>
      <p>He translates an engineering breakthrough into a bodily fact. That reduces perceived learning cost.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="frame shot">
    <img src="./assets/source-slides/slide-24.png" alt="Finger on iPhone touch screen" />

<div class="grid-2">

  <div>
    <div class="eyebrow">Substrate</div>
    <h2>OS X is the credibility bridge from toy to computer</h2>
    <div class="quote">“Desktop-class applications.”</div>
    <p>The software claim explains why the screen matters: the device is not merely touchable; it can run richer, adaptable applications.</p>
    <div class="analysis">
      <h3>Why it works</h3>
      <p>This upgrades the category from phone feature set to computing platform, which justifies the later ecosystem claims.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

---

<div class="grid-2">
  <div>
    <div class="eyebrow">Adoption bridge</div>
    <h2>iTunes makes the new device feel operationally familiar</h2>
    <p>After the radical interface claim, he lowers switching anxiety: millions already know how to sync an iPod.</p>
    <div class="analysis">
      <h3>Pattern</h3>
      <p>Alternate novelty with familiarity. Break the category, then show the user does not need to relearn everything.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="frame shot">

  <div>
    <div class="eyebrow">Object reveal</div>
    <h2>Industrial design is delayed until the argument is ready for it</h2>
    <p>By the time the object is described, the audience knows why the front has one button and why thinness matters.</p>
    <div class="analysis">
      <h3>Why it works</h3>
      <p>The device is not introduced as a beautiful artifact alone. Its form is the visible consequence of the interface thesis.</p>
    </div>
  </div>

<div class="chapter">
  <div class="rule"></div>
  <h1>The demo is built around verbs, not specs.</h1>
  <p class="text-2xl">Touch, scroll, swipe, pinch, call, merge, browse, zoom. The product becomes understandable as a chain of actions.</p>
</div>

---

<div class="grid-2">
  <div>
    <div class="eyebrow">Demo principle</div>
    <h2>He starts with the smallest possible magic trick: unlock</h2>
    <div class="quote">“Want to see that again?”</div>
    <p>The first demo is not a feature checklist. It is a physical proof that the interface is direct, safe, and repeatable.</p>
    <div class="analysis">
      <h3>Why it works</h3>
      <p>Repetition turns a tiny gesture into a shared event. The audience learns the interaction grammar before the apps arrive.</p>
    </div>
  </div>

  <div>
    <div class="eyebrow">Demo principle</div>
    <h2>The first iPod proof is not music. It is scrolling.</h2>
    <div class="quote">“I just take my finger, and I scroll.”</div>
    <p>The action compresses the whole thesis: no stylus, no keyboard, no mode switch, no instruction manual.</p>
    <div class="analysis">
      <h3>Why it matters</h3>
      <p>A mundane list becomes the strongest evidence that the phone has a new control language.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="grid-2">
  <div>
    <div class="eyebrow">Emotional pacing</div>
    <h2>He lets delight punctuate utility</h2>
    <p>Cover Flow and album art are not the market thesis. They create emotional surplus after the usability thesis is proven.</p>
    <div class="analysis">
      <h3>Move</h3>
      <p>After demonstrating control, he shifts into pleasure: it looks gorgeous, moves fluidly, and feels like a high-end object.</p>
    </div>

  <div>
    <div class="eyebrow">Interface lineage</div>
    <h2>He names the control breakthrough as the platform breakthrough</h2>
    <p>Mouse made Mac possible. Click wheel made iPod possible. Multi-touch makes iPhone possible.</p>
    <div class="analysis">
      <h3>What he is doing</h3>
      <p>This is a causal story, not a comparison table. The interface is framed as the unlock for a new product category.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="grid-2">
  <div>
    <div class="eyebrow">Phone demo</div>
    <h2>The first public call is staged as product proof and social proof</h2>
    <p>Calling Jony and Phil makes call management legible, but it also shows Apple’s own leaders using the device live.</p>
    <div class="analysis">
      <h3>Why it works</h3>
      <p>The audience sees interruption, hold, merge, private call, and hangup as one continuous story rather than separate features.</p>

  <div>
    <div class="eyebrow">Rebuttal by demonstration</div>
    <h2>The soft keyboard answers the obvious objection in context</h2>
    <p>Instead of arguing against plastic keyboards abstractly, he types a message while the audience watches correction and send flow.</p>
    <div class="analysis">
      <h3>What he is doing</h3>
      <p>He does not hide the risky part of the product. He puts it in a normal task and lets successful completion carry the argument.</p>

      <h3>Why it works</h3>
      <p>The gesture feels discoverable because it is shown on a familiar object: a photo someone wants to inspect.</p>

    <div class="eyebrow">Category escalation</div>
    <h2>Safari turns the phone into the Internet, not a mobile substitute</h2>
    <div class="quote">“Not a baby web browser.”</div>

    <div class="analysis">

    <div class="analysis">
      <h3>Pattern</h3>
      <p>Use partner logos late enough that they amplify the product thesis instead of substituting for it.</p>

      <h3>Effect</h3>
      <p>The questions make a one-way keynote feel conversational, and they convert understanding into audience response.</p>

    <h2>The repeated “first real...” phrasing upgrades features into category claims</h2>
    <p>He does not just say the phone has email, a browser, and maps. He frames them as first credible versions on a mobile device.</p>
    <div class="analysis">
      <h3>Why it works</h3>
      <p>Repetition creates a drumbeat of legitimacy: each feature is not another checkbox, it is another category correction.</p>

<h1>Reusable Playbook</h1>

<div class="grid-3 mt-8">
  <div>
    <div class="big-number">1</div>
    <h3>Draw the old world</h3>
    <p>Name the current compromise so every competitor inherits the same flaw.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <div class="big-number">2</div>
    <h3>Reveal the mechanism</h3>
    <p>Make the core invention feel like the simplest possible answer.</p>
  </div>
  <div>
    <div class="big-number">3</div>
    <h3>Prove through verbs</h3>
    <p>Let live actions carry the feature claims and the emotional payoff.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="analysis mt-10">
  <h3>For our own presentations</h3>
  <p>Lead with the category tension, not the product catalog. Use visuals to show the old constraint, then demos to make the new behavior obvious.</p>
</div>
